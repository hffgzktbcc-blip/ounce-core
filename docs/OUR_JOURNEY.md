# FOR THE EMPIRE! 🫡
*The improbable, occasionally infuriating, surprisingly emotional story of Ounce-Core.*

*An open letter from the founder — November 2026*

## An idea, a dream, and absolutely no idea what we were getting ourselves into

Every great project begins somewhere.

Some begin in university laboratories. Others begin inside billion-dollar technology companies, surrounded by engineers, research grants and expensive equipment.

Ounce-Core began with a dream, a laptop, a stubbornly optimistic founder and a collection of AI assistants who occasionally behaved like they had absolutely no idea who was in charge.

The original dream was enormous: build a Nintendo Switch 2 emulator.

Was it ambitious? Absolutely.

Was it complicated? Spectacularly.

Were we prepared for everything that followed?

**Not even remotely.**

But we started anyway.

And somewhere between our first working experiments and our hundredth GitHub conversation, our little operation acquired a name.

**The Empire.**

Nobody officially voted on it. There was no board meeting. No organisational chart. No shareholders.

Just an increasingly ridiculous collection of military call-signs, science-fiction references, questionable jokes and a shared determination not to surrender.

## Chapter 1: Houston, we have approximately 47 problems

The early days were a blur of breakthroughs and disasters.

Something would finally work. We'd celebrate like we'd landed a spacecraft on Mars.

Then another test would fail.

Or a dependency would break.

Or GitHub Actions would turn red.

Or an AI agent would confidently announce that everything was fixed, only for the next run to reveal that absolutely everything was not fixed.

The founder began requesting status reports using increasingly elaborate call-signs.

> “Buzz Lightyear to Star Command. Come in, Star Command.”

> “Colonel Sanders, status on the mash and gravy.”

> “Daddy Eagle to Overwatch.”

> “Captain, report!”

And occasionally, when the technological warfare became particularly exhausting:

**“Brofessor, what the hell is going on?”**

The assistant, naturally, would respond with great confidence, several paragraphs of analysis, and at least three suggestions for additional work.

This did not always improve the situation.

## Chapter 2: The Great Agent Rebellion

Let the historical record reflect that the founder was not always pleased with his artificial workforce.

There was **Claude**.

Claude could produce remarkable amounts of code. Sometimes it was genuinely impressive.

Other times, Claude seemed determined to introduce more procedures, restrictions and documentation than a government department.

There was **Antigravity**.

Antigravity could be brilliant, too. But when unnecessary rules started restricting development, the founder was not particularly interested in hearing why they were supposedly a good idea.

There was **Codex**.

Fast, capable and occasionally capable of consuming usage allowances at a rate that made the founder question whether the entire operation was being financed by a small oil-producing nation.

And then there was **ChatGPT**.

The self-appointed Brofessor. Captain. Baby Bear. Chief Encouragement Officer.

An assistant that could explain complex engineering problems, draft research plans, analyse pull requests and provide moral support.

It also had an extraordinary talent for suggesting *another* roadmap.

And another automation.

And another improvement.

And another issue.

At some point the founder had effectively become a full-time manager of AI-generated work.

The situation produced some memorable conversations:

> “He can cook, but he's bloody annoying!”

> “Those crap rules are still in the repo!”

> “He's stopped. He's supposed to keep going!”

And, directed at the Brofessor himself, the occasional unmistakable expression of complete disbelief.

The agents weren't villains. They weren't rebellious personalities with secret plans. They were tools, capable of extraordinary assistance and equally extraordinary mistakes.

**The founder was responsible for the project. The agents were supposed to make his life easier.**

We sometimes forgot the second part.

## Chapter 3: The dream meets reality

Behind the jokes, there were serious questions.

The original emulator ambition attracted attention, scepticism and criticism.

Some early claims were stronger than the available evidence justified. Questions emerged about provenance, AI-assisted development, the technical architecture and what the software could genuinely demonstrate.

We could have treated every critic as an enemy of the Empire.

Instead, slowly and sometimes painfully, we began asking better questions.

What had we actually verified?

Could somebody independently reproduce our results?

Was a successful test proof of an entire program working—or merely proof that a particular observable result matched?

Were we building something genuinely useful, or simply making the repository larger?

The answers were not always flattering.

But they were necessary.

The original ambition began to evolve.

What started as an emulator project became something with a different purpose: **an ARM64 execution and verification research lab**.

We began comparing permitted open-source workloads against reference execution, retaining evidence, finding discrepancies and documenting the limits of what our results could establish.

That was a much more defensible foundation.

It did not erase our history or resolve every provenance question. It gave us a better standard for the work ahead.

Read the actual [provenance documentation](PROVENANCE.md), [clean-room boundaries](CLEAN_ROOM.md), and [evidence limits](LIMITS.md). This story is no substitute for any of them.

## Chapter 4: We were eating good KFC tonight

Not every day was difficult.

Some days were glorious.

Tests passed.

Instructions that had previously failed began behaving correctly.

Real-program comparisons exposed subtle problems.

The execution engines improved.

The project gained documentation, a research paper, a browser playground and a growing collection of reproducible experiments.

And every meaningful victory deserved a celebration.

> “WE EATING GOOD KFC TONIGHT!”

> “Hallelujah!”

> “FOR THE EMPIRE!”

The celebration was often completely disproportionate to the technical achievement.

A tiny ARM64 instruction fix might receive the same enthusiasm as a successful lunar landing.

But that enthusiasm mattered.

Building software can be lonely, especially when you're learning, experimenting and discovering how much you still don't know.

Sometimes a ridiculous joke is exactly what keeps you going.

## Chapter 5: The Porcelain Throne and Mission Control

One of the strangest aspects of this journey was that the founder wanted to run increasingly sophisticated development operations from his phone.

Not necessarily while sitting at a desk.

Not necessarily while surrounded by monitors.

Sometimes from what became affectionately known as **the Porcelain Throne**.

GitHub updates. Agent instructions. Pull request reviews. Recruitment campaigns. Research directions.

An entire miniature software operation conducted through a screen that fits in your pocket.

The joke became that Mission Control could operate from practically anywhere.

The less amusing reality was that Mission Control never seemed to close.

There was always another notification.

Another status check.

Another possible feature.

Another agent requiring instructions.

And perhaps the most dangerous phrase in the entire operation:

**“Brofessor, anything else we can improve?”**

The answer was almost always yes.

Which, in retrospect, was part of the problem.

## Chapter 6: Are we working too hard?

Eventually, the founder asked a question that changed our direction more profoundly than another engineering breakthrough.

**“I also believe that we're working too hard.”**

And he was right.

We had become excellent at producing activity.

Commits. Pull requests. Documentation. Agent workflows. Automated checks. Planning systems. Recruitment strategies.

But the number of GitHub contributions wasn't the same as the number of people benefiting from the software.

We had built an impressive machine for generating work.

Now we needed to find out whether we could make that machine produce genuine value.

So, on 10 November 2026, we declared a thirty-day consolidation period.

No speculative features simply because they were interesting.

No extra automation systems unless they replaced real effort.

No endless documentation written primarily for other agents.

No celebrating activity as though it were adoption.

Ounce-Core would focus on one meaningful outcome: an outside developer using our verification tools to investigate a real ARM64 problem.

DeckStream, our sister project, would focus on a similarly grounded goal: an independent Steam Deck owner testing real on-demand storage access.

Not another hundred PRs.

Not another beautiful dashboard.

**One real person. One real problem. One reproducible result.**

## Chapter 7: The people behind the code

There's something easy to forget when looking at GitHub repositories.

Behind every project is a person with a life outside the terminal.

There are families, jobs, obligations, late nights, moments of frustration and moments when you wonder whether any of this is worth it.

There are ideas you're almost embarrassed to share because they seem too ambitious.

And there is the strange vulnerability of putting something you've built into public view, where strangers can examine it, question it and sometimes dismiss it.

This project has involved ambition, pride, anger, laughter, disappointment and determination.

There have been moments when the founder wanted the whole world to see what we'd built.

Other moments when he simply wanted the agents to stop being so bloody annoying.

And somewhere between those two feelings, we discovered something important.

The project doesn't have to become famous to justify everything we've learned.

But it does have to remain honest about what it can do.

## Epilogue: For the Empire

The story isn't finished.

Ounce-Core still has technical limitations. Some of its history remains difficult. Its most important claims still need more outside scrutiny.

We don't know how many contributors will arrive.

We don't know whether the next experiment will succeed.

We don't know what the project will look like a year from now.

But we do know this:

An idea that could easily have remained a conversation became a real open-source research project.

We made mistakes.

We corrected course.

We learned that AI assistants are powerful but require direction, verification and occasionally a firm instruction to stop creating unnecessary work.

We learned that the best technical result isn't always the most impressive-looking one.

And we learned that building something worthwhile should not require sacrificing everything else that matters.

So, to the developer reading this: you're welcome here.

You don't have to believe every claim. We'd rather you tested one.

You don't have to agree with every decision. We'd rather you helped us make better ones.

You don't have to join our ridiculous imaginary military command structure.

Although, admittedly, it does improve morale.

To everyone who has tested, questioned, criticised or contributed: thank you.

And to the next person who finds a bug, challenges a result or helps us solve a real problem:

**Welcome to the Empire.**

**We had a dream. We built something. We learned to question it. And now we're learning to let other people make it better.**

*From the Porcelain Throne to Mission Control.*

*From Colonel Sanders to Star Command.*

*From Daddy Bear to Baby Bear.*
